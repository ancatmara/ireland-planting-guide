ReferenceHow to read this plan▼

Plant references. Each plant is named with the code from its section heading in the guide, for example Apple (F2) or Hosta (S11). These are the codes on the plant headings (A1, B28, T36 and so on). The "Complete Reference Table" at the end of the guide uses the same codes. Where one section covers several species or cultivars (for example Tulip, B28), its rows share that section's code.

What is from the guide and what is not.

  • Anything unmarked comes from the guide: its planting seasons, feeding advice, pruning notes, hardiness warnings and "West of Ireland" notes.
  • † marks a timing or technique that the guide does not state, and which comes from standard UK and Irish horticultural practice (RHS guidance and similar). These are sensible defaults. They are best checked against plant labels and the supplier's advice.
  • Where the guide gives only a season (for example "spring"), the task is placed in the month that suits Galway conditions and marked †.

Galway city. This plan is written for a garden in Galway city. The city is on the coast, so it is generally milder than inland Co. Galway, but it is wet, windy and not frost-free. Where a task depends on frost risk, lean towards the later date if your garden is exposed or sits in a hollow where cold air collects.

Materials. Section 6 covers the materials named in the guide and a wider list of garden materials that the guide does not name (marked †, or described as named in plant cards where the guide does mention them), with the months in which each matters.

How much to do. The plan covers every plant in the guide, so no single garden will do everything. Skip entries for plants you do not grow.

ReferenceThe Galway climate, and what it means for the garden▼

What the data say

Feature What the sources show
Temperature Maritime and mild. Galway city averages about 6.8 °C in January and about 16 °C in July (Galway Tourism).
Rainfall About 1,100 mm a year in Galway city, compared with about 680 mm in Dublin. Rainfall in the west generally runs between 1,000 and 1,250 mm, and December and January are usually the wettest months.
Wet days Wet days are frequent. Ireland's average runs from about 151 a year on the east and south-east coasts to about 225 in parts of the west, so summer is not reliably dry.
Frost Galway city is coastal. Coastal areas have historically been generally frost-free until December, with the last air frost in late March or early April (Irish Times). Met Éireann's Galway (University College) station is closed, so no city-specific frost statistics are available from it. The nearest coastal reference, Mace Head, recorded no air frost at all in November 2020.
Cold snaps still happen Met Éireann issued Status Orange low-temperature and ice warnings covering Galway in the January 2025 cold snap (Galway City Council, RSA). Borderline plants can still be hit in a bad winter.
Warm spells happen too Connemara National Park reached 32.2 °C on 13 July 2025 (Met Éireann). Pots and new plantings can dry out fast even in the wet west.
Wind Mace Head on the Galway coast had the highest annual mean wind speed of Met Éireann's stations in 2025, at 26.9 km/h. Staking and shelter matter.

What this means in practice

  1. Drainage decides success or failure. The guide's first rule applies all year: improve drainage before planting. Winter wet kills far more plants here than winter cold.
  2. Work the clay at the right time. Never dig, plant into or walk on saturated clay. It compacts and smears, and it takes years to recover. A handful that crumbles is ready to work. A handful that rolls into a sticky sausage is not. †
  3. Two good planting windows. Early autumn (soil still warm, rain returning) and spring (from late March, once the clay is workable) suit most plants. Midwinter planting into cold, waterlogged clay is the riskiest time. †
  4. Wind. Stake dwarf-rootstock fruit trees permanently (the guide is firm on this), and stake tall perennials and annuals before they need it.
  5. Slugs and snails. Mild, damp weather means they are active for most of the year. The guide names hostas, lupins and similar soft growth as the main victims.
  6. Fungal problems in damp summers. Expect mildew (zinnia is named in the guide) and blight on tomatoes. Airflow, shelter from rain and prompt removal of affected growth help. †
  7. Frost is a window, not a date. Treat all "after the last frost" dates as approximate, and keep fleece to hand until late May.

Planting and soil

  • Planting hole: at least twice the width and depth of the rootball. Mix the excavated clay 50/50 with multipurpose compost, then add 20–30% horticultural grit by volume.
  • Mulch: horticultural bark, 5–7 cm deep, kept away from stems and crowns.
  • Test the soil pH before planting. The guide notes that west of Ireland soils vary considerably. Retest each spring if you grow acid-lovers. †
  • Ericaceous plants (blueberry, cranberry, lingonberry, heathers, camellia, skimmia, prickly heath, deer fern and similar) get ericaceous compost and ericaceous feed. Never add lime nearby.
  • Other materials: well-rotted manure, leaf mould, sharp sand, John Innes composts, fleece, supports and slug controls all have a place in a Galway garden. Section 6 lists them by use and by month.
  • Containers: pebbles for the drainage layer, perlite at 10–20% in potting mixes, and grit-rich mixes for rosemary, lavender, thyme and alpines. Stand pots on feet so they never sit in water. †

The nesting-season ban. Under Section 40 of the Wildlife Act 1976 (as amended), it is an offence to cut, burn or otherwise destroy hedge vegetation between 1 March and 31 August, with limited exemptions such as road safety. BirdWatch Ireland states that the ban applies to private gardens as much as to farms. Do all hedge cutting in September to February. Check any dense shrub or hedge for nests before pruning in spring or summer.

Feeding rules

  • Most feeds should stop by midsummer to August, so that wood ripens before winter. The guide gives specific stop dates: apple (after July), plum and peach (midsummer), wisteria (midsummer), Japanese maple (after July), roses and clematis (late August), hosta and kiwi (August), hydrangea (August), camellia (midsummer).
  • Tomato feed is the guide's standard high-potash feed for fruiting and flowering plants.
  • Avoid high-nitrogen feeds on plants grown for flowers or fruit. The guide repeats this for raspberries on wet clay, wisteria, clematis, honeysuckle, lilac and others.

Safety and legal points

  • Wear gloves for Colchicum corms (B18, highly toxic), Monkshood (S13), Winter Aconite (B29), Yellow Flag Iris (W25), Water Crowfoot (W7), Chinese Lantern (P100), and thorny plants: Sea Buckthorn (F22) and Japanese Barberry (T21).
  • Keep away from children, pets and livestock: holly berries, Irish yew, snowdrops, daffodils, bluebells, fritillaria, monkshood, lily of the valley and the other plants in the guide's toxic list.
  • Water Hyacinth (W100) is excluded from this plan. The guide states it is banned and must not be bought or grown.
  • Fringed Water Lily (W10) is legally restricted in the Republic of Ireland as well. The guide mentions only Northern Ireland. Invasives Ireland lists Nymphoides peltata among the species restricted under Regulations 49 and 50 of S.I. No. 477/2011 (the Third Schedule list kept by the National Parks and Wildlife Service, NPWS), applying "throughout the State". Regulation 49 makes it an offence to plant, disperse or cause such a plant to grow. At least one plant retailer states that the listing does not prevent gardeners from growing it, which conflicts with the NPWS listing. The cautious course is not to plant it. Where a plant is already present, the NPWS can confirm the position. This is general information, not legal advice.
  • Water Soldier (W28): listed in Schedule 9 of the Wildlife (Northern Ireland) Order 1985, where it is an offence to plant it or cause it to grow in the wild. It does not appear on the Invasives Ireland list of plants restricted in the Republic under Regulations 49 and 50 of S.I. No. 477/2011, but restrictions can change, so the NPWS is the place to confirm the current position before planting. No part of it should ever escape into open water.

The monthly plan

JanuaryMonth 1

Galway conditions. The coldest and one of the wettest months. Mean temperature is about 6–7 °C, daylight is short, and the clay is usually saturated. Frosts are usually lighter in the city than inland, but a hard cold snap can still happen (Met Éireann Status Orange low-temperature warnings covered Galway in January 2025). This is a month for protecting, pruning and planning, not for digging.

Priorities: (1) stay off wet clay, (2) check stakes and winter protection after gales, (3) prune what is meant to be pruned now, and (4) check stored tubers and overwintering pots.

Prune and tidy (dry, frost-free days only)

  • Apple (F2) † and Pear (F16): winter prune while dormant. Trained forms such as espalier and cordon are pruned in summer instead.
  • Wisteria (C16) †: shorten the shoots you cut back last summer to two or three buds (RHS: January or February).
  • Climbing roses (C6) and shrub roses (T36) †: tie in climbers and remove dead and crossing wood. Hold the main prune of shrub roses until February or March.
  • Hardy Grapes (C8) †: prune now while fully dormant, ideally before the sap rises.
  • Hazelnut (F11): traditional late-winter pruning. Finish by February.
  • Do not prune any Prunus: Almond (F1), Plum (F8, F17), Peach (F15), cherries (F24, F26, T24). The guide says summer only.
  • Do not prune Forsythia (T5), Deutzia (T15), Mock Orange (T28) or Lilac (T29 †) now. They flower on old wood, so wait until they have flowered.
  • Witch Hazel (T41): leave alone while it flowers.

Protect, check and store

  • Check every stake and tie on dwarf-rootstock fruit trees (Apple F2, Plum F8, Pear F16) and young trees such as Black Locust (T4). Permanent staking is essential against Atlantic winds.
  • Tree ferns (Tasmanian S23, Rough S18, Silver S20): keep the crown wrapped with fleece or straw in hard frost. Check that the wrap is not sodden.
  • Borderline plants left in the ground: keep thick dry mulch over Pineapple Lily (B21), Agapanthus (B2), River Lily (B23), Alstroemeria (P1), Society Garlic (P41) and Dwarf Sword Lily (B9), where you have left them in.
  • Stored tender bulbs and tubers (Acidanthera B1, Ixia B5, Freesia B11, Iron Cross B15, Ranunculus B22, Dwarf Sword Lily B9): look for mould or shrivelling. Keep them dry and frost-free. †
  • Overwintering pots under cover: Lemon Verbena (H17, keep on the dry side), Lemongrass (H18), Norfolk Island Pine (T34, bright and cool at about 17 °C, no feeding), and any Olive (F14), Pomegranate (F18) or Golden Mimosa (T17) in containers. Water sparingly.
  • Pots outdoors: make sure they stand on feet and are not full of water. Wet roots kill borderline plants faster than frost does.

Enjoy Winter Aconite (B29, January to February), Snowdrops (B25), Cyclamen coum (B27, midwinter), Hellebore (S10), Witch Hazel (T41), Winter Heath (G6, Erica carnea) and the first Golden Mimosa (T17) in a mild spot.

Pond. If the pond freezes, melt a small hole with a pan of hot water so gases can escape. Do not hammer the ice. †

Plan and order. Check seed stocks. Order tubers and corms for spring planting (Ranunculus B22, Acidanthera B1, Freesia B11, Ixia B5). Order bare-root fruit and roses if you have not yet planted them.

If it is too wet. Do nothing to the soil. Pruning, tidying, repairs and ordering all work in the rain.

FebruaryMonth 2

Galway conditions. Still wet and cool (mean about 6 °C), but the days are noticeably longer. The first dry spells can arrive. Early blossom is already at risk.

Priorities: (1) the February fertiliser round for fruit trees, (2) finishing winter pruning, (3) cutting back old foliage, and (4) first sowings under cover.

Feed (all as stated in the guide)

  • Balanced fruit-tree fertiliser (Growmore or a specialist feed) every February: Apple (F2), Garden Plum (F8), Almond (F1), Peach (F15) and Pear (F16). Plum 'Aprimira' (F17) in the ground is also fed each February. Apple also gets bonemeal in the planting hole at planting.
  • Sulphate of potash in late winter: Redcurrant (F20) and Gooseberry (F9, late winter or early spring).
  • Conifer feed: Juniper 'Repanda' (G11) and Dwarf Mountain Pine (T10) get an occasional dressing of Kieserite or organic conifer feed in February or March. Newly planted ones wait 6–8 weeks after planting.

Prune and tidy

  • Kiwi 'Jenny' (C11): late-winter pruning to establish the framework.
  • Fig (F7): shape in late winter or early spring.
  • Elder 'Black Lace' (T11): for the biggest, darkest leaves, cut hard back to a framework now (this sacrifices some flowers). Prune more lightly if you want flowers and berries.
  • Epimedium (S7): cut old, tatty foliage hard back just before the flower spikes emerge.
  • Hellebore (S10): clear old foliage before the early-spring feed.
  • Deciduous ornamental grasses (P28): cut hard back to a few centimetres above the ground before new growth starts. Pampas Grass: cut back hard now or in early spring, wearing thick gloves and long sleeves.
  • Wisteria (C16) †: finish the winter prune by the end of February.
  • Raspberry (F19) †: if yours are autumn-fruiting, cut all canes to the ground now. Summer-fruiting types are pruned after harvest (August), so leave them.
  • Blueberry (F5) †: a light late-winter tidy, removing the oldest and weakest stems.
  • Shrub and climbing roses (T36, C6) †: main prune from mid-February into March.
  • Hedges (T7, T18, T20, T21): last chance before the ban begins on 1 March. Finish all trimming this month.
  • Ivy (C10) and Virginia Creeper (C15) †: trim back now, before nesting begins.

Sow and plant (under cover or in a frost-free spot †)

  • Ranunculus (B22): plant in pots from February to April. Soak the tubers for 2–4 hours first, with the claws pointing down, 5–8 cm deep.
  • Sweet Peas (A19): sow in pots under cover. † The guide allows spring or autumn sowing.
  • Garlic (H13): if you started cloves in modules in a cold frame over winter (the guide's suggestion for heavy clay), plant them out in early spring when the soil is workable.
  • Snowdrops "in the green" (B25) and Winter Aconite (B29): buy and plant just after flowering, with the leaves still on. This is more reliable than dry bulbs.

Protect

  • Almond (F1) flowers as early as February, so blossom is very vulnerable. Have fleece ready for frosty nights.
  • Magnolias (T30), Peach (F15) and Plum (F8) will follow in March and April. Avoid east-facing positions where morning sun hits frosted flowers.

If it is too wet. Prune, order, tidy pots and sharpen tools. Do not plant into saturated clay.

MarchMonth 3

Galway conditions. Mean about 7–8 °C. Soils warm slowly, and clay stays cold and wet for much of the month. Late frosts remain possible into early or mid-April. Wind and heavy showers are common. The hedge-cutting ban starts on 1 March.

Priorities: (1) first plantings and sowings once the clay is workable, (2) the main spring feed, (3) the last big pruning jobs, and (4) slug defence from the first shoots.

Sow and plant

  • Cornflower, Poppies, Pot Marigold, Poached Egg Plant, California Poppy, Love-in-a-Mist and Quaking Grass (A5, A14, A15, A13, A3, A11, A9): direct sow where they are to flower once the soil is workable (March if the clay is workable, otherwise April †). Poppies need light, so barely cover the seed. California Poppy and Love-in-a-Mist dislike root disturbance.
  • Half-hardy annuals under cover: Cockscomb (A4), Globe Amaranth (A8), Lambs Tails (A100), Strawflower (A17), Zinnia (A21), Snapdragons (A16), Drumstick Flower (A7), Winged Everlasting (A20). Sow in March or April † and plant out only after frost risk has passed.
  • Basil (H2): sow under cover. Keep it in pots on a warm sill, patio or in a greenhouse, because direct sowing outdoors is unreliable here.
  • Peas (H24): on heavy, cold clay the guide recommends starting seed in modules under cover. Sow successionally every few weeks from now into early summer.
  • Radish, Rocket, Spring Onions, Green Onions, Mustard Greens (H26, H27, H101, H14, H100): first sowings under a cloche or fleece † as the soil allows. Garden Cress (H12) can be sown on a windowsill.
  • Ragged Robin (W20): surface-sow in March or April.
  • Asparagus (H1) †: plant crowns in a trench 15–20 cm deep in a raised bed, backfilling gradually as shoots grow. Harvest nothing in years one and two.
  • Rhubarb (F21) †: plant or divide crowns with buds 1–2 cm below the soil.
  • Strawberries (F25) †: plant with the crown exactly at soil level (too deep rots, too shallow dries).
  • Ixia (B5) and Freesia (B11): plant in pots in March or April, 8–10 cm and 5–8 cm deep respectively.
  • Lilies (B16, B17): the guide allows early-spring planting, 15 cm deep on a bed of grit.
  • Horseradish (H102): root cuttings or crowns in spring, in a large, deep container to control its spread.
  • Hops (C9) †: plant rhizomes 5–8 cm deep.
  • Chives (H5), Mint (H19, H25): divide chives every 2–3 years. Repot mint into fresh compost each year (guide) and keep it in a container at least 30 cm wide and deep. †

Prune and tidy

  • Roses (T36, C6) †: finish pruning by mid-March, before the first feed.
  • Clematis (C3) †: Group 3 (late-flowering) cut hard to a pair of buds near the base. Group 2 (large-flowered early) tidy lightly and remove dead stems. Group 1 (including C. montana) wait until after flowering. Evergreen types need only tidying after flowering.
  • Witch Hazel (T41): once it has flowered, remove only dead, diseased or crossing wood. Avoid hard pruning.
  • Eucalyptus (T6) †: the guide says gardeners stool or coppice every year or two to keep the rounded juvenile foliage. Late March to April is the usual time.
  • Bamboo (T3) †: remove dead and old canes.
  • Grasses (P28): finish cutting back deciduous types. Comb evergreen types lightly.
  • Heathers (G6) †: shear Calluna in March or April, just below the old flower heads and never into bare wood. Erica carnea is trimmed after flowering.
  • Passion Flower (C1) †: if frost has cut it back (the guide says this can happen), wait for new growth to show before removing dead stems.
  • Do not cut hedges, or vegetation in dense shrubs where birds may nest, from now until September.

Feed and mulch (guide timings)

  • Roses (T36, C6): specialist rose fertiliser in late March.
  • Early-spring balanced fertiliser (Growmore or similar): Blackberries (F3), Raspberry (F19), Strawberry (F25), Serviceberry (F23), Honeyberry (F12), Forsythia (T5), Deutzia (T15), Mock Orange (T28), Japanese Flowering Cherry (T24), Sweet and Sour Cherry (F26, F24), Lilac (T29, superphosphate or balanced), Clematis (C3, slow-release), Kiwi (C11, as buds swell), Honeysuckle (C4), Hardy Grapes (C8, balanced with potassium), Rhubarb (F21), Delphiniums (P12), Hosta (S11, slow-release), Hellebore (S10, slow-release), Creeping Phlox (G4, slow-release).
  • High-nitrogen fertiliser: Blackcurrant (F4) in early spring, and Bamboo (T3) and Hops (C9) each spring.
  • Ericaceous feed: Heathers (G6) in March or April, and Camellia (T22) after flowering. Skimmia (T26) and Prickly Heath (G15) in spring.
  • Peonies (P31): balanced fertiliser as the red shoots emerge.
  • Magnolias (T30): balanced or ericaceous slow-release in spring.
  • Mulch with bark or compost once the soil has warmed a little and is moist, ideally in April on cold clay. †

Protect

  • Slug defence from the first shoots: Hosta (S11, wool pellets, copper tape on pots or nematodes), Lupins (P26, protect emerging growth), Delphiniums (P12 †).
  • Blossom frost cover: Peach (F15) blossoms March to April, so protect with fleece on frosty nights. Magnolias (T30) and plums (F8, F17) are also at risk.
  • Cats and young Catnip (H3): a cloche or wire cage protects new plants.
  • Tree ferns (S23, S18, S20): leave the wrap on until the hard frosts are clearly over, then unwrap gradually. †

Divide and move (when the clay is crumbly) †: Hostas (S11), Daylilies (P11, every 3–4 years), Avens (P2, P7, every 2–3 years), Heuchera (S4, every 3 years, spring or autumn), Helenium (P39), Bearded Iris (P19, hold until after flowering).

Enjoy Daffodils (B7), Crocus (B6), Hyacinth (B14), Dwarf Iris (B8), Siberian Squill (B24), Primroses (S17, including the garden hybrid primroses, from late winter), Marsh Marigold (W17), Umbrella Plant (W23, March to April), Almond (F1) and Peach (F15) blossom, and Magnolias (T30).

If it is too wet. Sow in modules under cover, and prune, tie in and feed. Wait to plant out until the clay crumbles in the hand.

AprilMonth 4

Galway conditions. Mean about 9–10 °C. The soil is warming and growth takes off, but frost is not finished: coastal areas typically lose it by about mid-April. April can swing from warm, sunny spells to a sharp night frost, so keep fleece handy.

Priorities: (1) the main planting month for hardy plants in the ground, (2) sowing, (3) starting the regular feeding schedule, and (4) pruning spring-flowering shrubs as soon as the flowers fade.

Sow and plant

  • Hardy annuals, direct: Borage (A1, 1 cm deep), Sweet Alyssum (A18, 3 mm), Phacelia (A10, spring through summer, for pollinators or as a green manure), Poached Egg Plant (A13), Cornflower (A5), Pot Marigold (A15) and Poppies (A14).
  • Sweet Peas (A19): plant out hardened-off plants against canes, a trellis or wigwam. †
  • Perennial vegetables: Asparagus crowns (H1) and Rhubarb (F21) if not already planted.
  • Peas (H24), Radish (H26), Rocket (H27), Spring Onions (H101, H14), Mustard Greens (H100), Parsley (H22 †), Coriander and Dill (H8, H11): sow in short rows every few weeks. Coriander and Dill bolt if overfed, so feed little.
  • Tender bulbs, in pots or the ground after frost risk (from about mid-April †): Acidanthera (B1, 10–16 cm on a bed of sharp sand, April to May), Iron Cross (B15, 5 cm, April to May), Dwarf Sword Lily (B9, 10–16 cm), Ixia (B5) and Freesia (B11) if not yet planted, and Pineapple Lily (B21, 10–15 cm, after frost).
  • River Lily (B23): plant rhizomes at soil level in spring.
  • Kiwi 'Jenny' (C11): plant in April or May once frost risk has passed. Give it a sheltered, sun-warmed wall.
  • Japanese Anemone (P20): plant potted rhizomes in spring. Siberian Iris (P19): at soil level or 2–3 cm below, in spring or early autumn.
  • Container fruit: Blueberry (F5), Cranberry (F6) and Lingonberry (F13) in ericaceous compost with plenty of perlite and drainage. Pot on or refresh the top layer now. †
  • Lawn alternatives: White Clover (G22) can be sown direct, and Chamomile (H4) seed is surface-sown in spring.
  • Pond (from late April): † plant marginals such as Marsh Marigold (W17), Bogbean (W5), Flowering Rush (W9), Arrowhead (W1), Mare's Tail (W16, in a basket), Yellow Flag Iris (W25, in a basket), Water Forget-me-not (W24) and Bog Pimpernel (W4). Push aquatic fertiliser tablets into the basket compost at the start of the growing season.
  • Water Violet (W29): plant in spring or autumn, then leave it undisturbed through summer.

Prune and tidy

  • Forsythia (T5): prune immediately after flowering, then mulch. Never prune in autumn or winter.
  • Hydrangea macrophylla (T32) †: remove last year's flower heads down to a pair of healthy buds once the worst frosts are over. Leave the old heads on until then, as frost protection.
  • Heathers (G6): finish shearing Calluna. Trim Erica carnea straight after flowering. †
  • Rockcress (G17): trim lightly after flowering.
  • Chocolate Vine (C2): after flowering, prune hard if needed. It is very vigorous and needs at least one hard prune a year.
  • Clematis montana (C3, Group 1) †: prune after flowering, in late April or May.
  • Epimedium (S7), Hellebore (S10): finish tidying. Hellebore gets its annual compost mulch after flowering (guide).
  • Hops (C9) †: train the new shoots up the support.

Feed and mulch

  • Ericaceous liquid feed from April to August: Blueberry (F5, fortnightly), Cranberry (F6, monthly), Lingonberry (F13, monthly). Heathers (G6, an ericaceous fertiliser in March or April, or a liquid feed such as Phostrogen April to September). Use only ericaceous feeds, and no general-purpose feeds on cranberry.
  • Monthly liquid feeds start: Hosta (S11, April through midsummer), Japanese Maple in containers (T25, April to July), and Clematis (C3: high-potash tomato feed fortnightly from April to August).
  • Slow-release spring feeds (if not yet done): Japanese Maple (T25), Hosta (S11), Hydrangea (T32), Magnolias (T30), Camellia (T22, after flowering).
  • Hydrangea macrophylla (T32): one spring feed. For blue flowers use a feed low in phosphorus and high in potassium, with ericaceous compost. Stop feeding after August.
  • Bark mulch 5–7 cm around established plants, once the soil is warm and moist. Keep it off stems and crowns. †

Protect

  • Frost fleece on blossom at night (peach, plum, cherry, pear: F15, F8, F17, F24, F26, F16). Remove it by day so pollinators can reach the flowers. †
  • Slugs and snails: peak danger for Hosta (S11), Lupins (P26), Delphiniums (P12 †).
  • Tomato and tender plant hardening: begin hardening off half-hardy annuals and tomatoes in a sheltered spot by day. †

Harvest and enjoy

  • Rhubarb (F21) †: pull stems from established crowns, but not in the first year after planting.
  • Wild Garlic (H32): leaves and flowers from April to June. Check identity by crushing a leaf: it must smell strongly of garlic, because the leaves can be confused with Lily of the Valley and Lords-and-Ladies.
  • Scurvy Grass (H30), Common Sorrel (H7), Chives (H5): snip leaves sparingly.
  • In flower: Tulips (B28), Fritillaria meleagris (B12), Grape Hyacinth (B13), Bluebells (B19, late April), Wood Anemone (B3), Primulas (S17), Forget-me-nots (S8), Bleeding Heart (S1), Lungwort (P25), Magnolias (T30), Camellia (T22), Skimmia (T26), Japanese Flowering Cherry (T24, mid-spring) and Hawthorn blossom (T7, May).

Leave alone. Let daffodil, bluebell, tulip and other bulb foliage yellow naturally. Daffodil foliage needs at least six weeks before removal (guide).

Pond and bog. Marsh Marigold (W17) in flower. Avoid disturbing the pond bed while frogspawn is present. †

If it is too wet. Pot up in the greenhouse or porch, hold off planting out, and put fleece or cloches over sown rows.

OctoberMonth 10

Galway conditions. Mean about 11 °C, with frequent rain, gales and falling leaves. Coastal areas such as Galway city have historically stayed generally frost-free until December, though a clear, still night can bring an early frost †. This is the key month for bringing tender plants in and for planting the last bulbs.

Priorities: (1) rescue tender plants before the first frost, (2) plant alliums and garlic, (3) lift tender bulbs, and (4) mulch and tidy.

Bring in or protect (before the first frost)

  • Pots to move under cover: Lemon Verbena (H17, keep on the dry side), Lemongrass (H18), Norfolk Island Pine (T34, bright and cool at about 17 °C, with no feeding over winter), Slipperwort (A2), Pickle Plant (G14), Cushion Bush (T8), and container-grown Olive (F14), Pomegranate (F18), Golden Mimosa (T17), Agapanthus (B2), Society Garlic (P41) and Pineapple Lily (B21).
  • Egyptian Papyrus (W8) and Umbrella Grass (W22): lift and move to a frost-free place.
  • Tree ferns (S23, S18, S20): prepare fleece or straw for crown wrapping. Wrap in November, or earlier if a frost is forecast.
  • Overwintering wet-sensitive plants (Rosemary H28, Lavender H15, Sage H29, French Tarragon H31 in pots): move pots to a sheltered, dry spot. The guide says these herbs struggle most with winter wet on clay.

Lift and store

  • Acidanthera (B1): lift after the first frosts, dry and store frost-free.
  • Freesia (B11), Ixia (B5), Iron Cross (B15), Ranunculus (B22): lift after the foliage dies down and store dry and frost-free, or keep the pots dry under cover.
  • Dwarf Sword Lily (B9): in colder, more exposed spots lift when the foliage dies back. In milder gardens a thick dry mulch is often enough.
  • Pineapple Lily (B21): lift, or mulch thickly if left in the ground.

Plant

  • Alliums (B20): plant mid to late autumn, 2–3 times the bulb's depth (about 20 cm for large bulbs, 8–10 cm for small ones). Alliums do very well in pots on wet clay.

  • Sweet Peas (A19): sow in pots under cover for earlier, stronger plants next year.
  • Container-grown trees, shrubs and perennials †: still a good window while the soil is warm and moist.
  • Heather (G6), Hellebore (S10), Epimedium (S7), Hepatica (S19) and other woodland plants: autumn planting suits them. †

Prune and tidy

  • Asparagus (H1): cut back the ferny growth when it turns yellow, then top-dress with compost.
  • Hops (C9) †: cut the bines back once they have died down.
  • Fig (F7) †: remove any fruit larger than a pea. Keep the pea-sized embryo figs, which survive winter and ripen next year.
  • Leave seed heads standing on Coneflower (P9, for seed-eating birds), Border Sedum (P6, structure), Feather Reed Grass (P28) and Statice for winter interest.
  • Cut back collapsed foliage and diseased leaves (rose blackspot, mildewed growth). † Do not compost badly diseased material.
  • Cut back Meadowsweet (W19) and other spent bog plants if you did not do so earlier.
  • Climbing and shrub roses (C6, T36) †: shorten very long stems to reduce wind rock before the gales.

Mulch

  • Rhubarb (F21): generous well-rotted manure or compost mulch after the leaves die back. The guide calls this the most beneficial treatment.

  • Daffodil drifts (B7), Snowdrops (B25), Cyclamen coum (B27): compost or leaf mould mulch.
  • Bamboo (T3): top-dress with compost each autumn (guide).

Harvest. Apples and Pears (F2, F16), Hazelnuts (F11), Sea Buckthorn (F22), Rowan berries (T33, for the birds), Autumn Raspberries (F19), Hardy Grapes (C8), Kiwi (C11 †), Horseradish roots (H102 †, dig after the leaves die back) and Cranberry (F6 †).

NovemberMonth 11

Galway conditions. Mean about 8 °C, short and very wet days, and gales. Clear nights bring frost. Clay is heavy and sticky. This is the start of the bare-root season, but only on days when the soil is not waterlogged.

Priorities: (1) plant tulips and bare-root fruit trees, (2) start winter pruning, (3) protect tender plants and check stakes before the gales, and (4) clean up.

Plant

  • Garden hybrid Tulips (B28): plant in November, 20 cm deep (three times the bulb's height), which lowers the risk of tulip fire. Species tulips can go in a little earlier. For pots, the guide's reference table suggests a 25–30 cm container of 10 L or more. Plan to lift hybrids after the foliage dies back next summer, or treat them as annuals.
  • Garlic (H13): finish planting by the end of the month.
  • Bare-root fruit trees and bushes †: plant when the clay is workable. Do not plant into standing water.
    • Apple, Plum, Pear (F2, F8, F16): graft union at or just above soil level, planted to the nursery soil mark. Stake permanently.
    • Blackcurrant (F4): plant 5–7 cm deeper than the nursery mark, then cut all stems to 10 cm.
    • Gooseberry (F9): roots covered by about 5 cm, then cut all stems to 30 cm.
    • Redcurrant (F20): roots covered by about 5 cm.
    • Raspberry (F19): crown 5–7 cm below soil, then cut canes to 25 cm.
    • Roses (T36, C6): graft union just below soil level.
    • Hawthorn hedging whips (T7) †: a cheap, wind-hardy shelterbelt.
  • Heel in bare-root plants in a trench of free-draining soil if the clay is too wet to plant. †

Prune and tidy

  • Apple (F2) † and Pear (F16): winter pruning can begin now, on dry days.
  • Redcurrant, Gooseberry, Blackcurrant (F20, F9, F4) †: winter pruning from now to February.
  • Climbing roses (C6) †: prune and tie in.
  • Do not prune Prunus (guide).
  • Clear leaves from borders, pond and paths, and remove any diseased growth. †

Protect

  • Wrap tree ferns (S23, S18, S20) with fleece or straw around the crown if hard frost is forecast. Check that wrapping does not stay wet.
  • Mulch tender perennials where left in the ground: River Lily (B23, dry winter mulch), Alstroemeria (P1, dry winter mulch), Agapanthus (B2), Pineapple Lily (B21).
  • Check all stakes and ties on young trees, dwarf-rootstock fruit trees, Black Locust (T4, brittle in wind) and tall climbers. Tie in loose growth. †
  • Pots outdoors: group them against a wall, raise them on feet, and wrap if frost is forecast. †
  • Slugs remain active in mild spells, so protect emerging bulb shoots and tender growth. †

Mulch. Apply bark mulch 5–7 cm deep to new plantings, keeping it off stems and crowns. Apply compost or manure mulch to fruit bushes and rhubarb if not already done.

Harvest. Kiwi (C11 †), late Apples and Pears, Horseradish (H102 †), Parsley, winter salad crops under cover, and the last Hardy Grapes.

Enjoy. Autumn colour, winter berries (Skimmia T26, Prickly Heath G15, Holly T18), and the first Winter Heath (G6) and Witch Hazel (T41) flowers.

Pond and bog. Remove the net once the leaves have fallen. Remove dead foliage and any rotting debris from the bottom. † Do not remove all vegetation, because the pond needs overwintering cover.

Tools and kit †. Clean and oil tools, wash and store spare pots, and sort grit, bark and compost stocks.

If it is too wet. Heel in, sort and clean, and prune on dry days. Do not struggle with a waterlogged bed.

DecemberMonth 12

Galway conditions. Mean about 6–7 °C. Along with January, one of the two wettest months, with the shortest days and strong gales. The clay is at its heaviest.

Priorities: (1) look after the garden's structure and protection, (2) plan, and (3) enjoy winter interest.

Plant (only if the clay is workable)

  • Bare-root fruit trees, bushes and roses †: as for November. Do not plant in waterlogged or frozen ground. Heel plants in until conditions improve.
  • Last Tulips (B28): if still unplanted and the soil is workable.

Prune and tidy

  • Hardy Grapes (C8) †: prune now while dormant, from December to January.
  • Apple (F2) † and Pear (F16): winter pruning on dry, frost-free days.
  • Currants and Gooseberries (F4, F20, F9) †.
  • Never prune stone fruit (Almond, Plum, Peach, cherries): summer only.

Protect and check

  • Tree fern crowns (S23, S18, S20) wrapped and dry. Check again after each frost.
  • Stakes and ties after each gale.
  • Overwintering pots under cover: Lemon Verbena (H17), Lemongrass (H18), Norfolk Island Pine (T34, bright, about 17 °C, no feeding), and any Olive, Pomegranate and Golden Mimosa in containers. Water sparingly.
  • Stored bulbs and tubers (B1, B5, B9, B11, B15, B22): inspect for rot or drying out. †
  • Snow or wet weight on conifers and evergreens †: shake it off gently.

Pond. Keep a small hole in any ice, using warm water. Do not smash the ice. †

Christmas greenery. Holly (T18) berries are toxic, so keep sprigs away from children and pets. Norfolk Island Pine (T34) makes a good indoor "Christmas tree" if kept bright and cool. In the garden, the guide calls Balsam Fir (T2) the archetypal Christmas tree and Fraser Fir (T14) a fragrant conifer specimen.

Enjoy. Witch Hazel (T41), Winter Heath (G6), Hellebore (S10), Skimmia (T26) and Prickly Heath (G15) berries, Cyclamen coum (B27), early Snowdrops (B25), Holly (T18) and Ivy (C10).

Plan. Review what worked. Note where water stood and which plants suffered, then plan raised beds, grit or containers for those spots next year. Order seeds and tubers.

If it is too wet. Stay indoors. The garden will wait.

Soil pH test kit Check acidity Autumn before planting; each spring for acid-lovers The guide says to test pH before planting. A kit or meter is the simple way.
Secateurs, loppers, pruning saw and gloves Pruning and handling As in the monthly plan Clean sharp tools make clean cuts. Gloves are needed for the toxic and thorny plants listed in section 3.
